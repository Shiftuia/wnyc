Listing sites show price and photos. They do not show whether you can get out of the neighbourhood. For a wheelchair user that is the whole question.

    1. Someone moving for a job

      “I got an offer in Midtown. I use a power chair. Where do I even start looking?” One sentence in, three neighbourhoods out — each with its accessible station, how often the elevator really works, and the biggest risk.

    2. Housing navigators and caseworkers

      Independent-living centres help clients find homes every week. The ranked table and its reasons can be read by a screen reader, sorted, and checked against the data.

    3. Advocates and planners

      The “accessibility deserts” view shows the affordable neighbourhoods that drop off the map — where one new elevator would open up the most homes.

          Proxies, not guarantees

          • Rent is Zillow’s typical market rent for the area (ZORI, Oct 2026), across all unit sizes. It is not the rent of an accessible apartment.
          • Housing figures are proxies: buildings from 1991 on fall under Fair Housing Act design rules, and elevator buildings can be step-free. Neither confirms an accessible unit exists or is vacant.
          • “One-seat accessible ride” means an accessible station near home and one near work share a subway line. There is no door-to-door routing.
          • Ride-hail waits come from completed trips only (Sept 2026). Requests that never found a car are not in the data.
          • Plan B fares are the typical Uber/Lyft price between two zones in Sept 2026, before tip. The monthly figure assumes 22 workdays and a round trip by ride-hail on each day the home-station elevator is out as often as it was from April to October 2026 — an estimate, not a bill.
          • Curb ramps come from a 2019–2021 city survey and may have changed.

          Data sources
